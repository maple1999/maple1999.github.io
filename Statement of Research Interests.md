# Statement of Research Interests

My research interests lie in **multimodal representation learning and reasoning**, particularly in understanding how models learn, organize, and utilize information to reason about complex and evolving environments. While recent advances in vision-language models have substantially improved their ability to interpret visual content, important questions remain about how these capabilities emerge and what limits their generalization. I am interested in going beyond improvements on individual tasks to investigate the underlying representation, learning, and computational mechanisms that enable models to reason reliably across different contexts. My long-term goal is to contribute methods and insights that can inform the development of more capable and generalizable multimodal models.

My research background is in temporal representation learning and efficient video-language understanding. My first-author work, *Count What Repeats*, accepted to CVPR 2026 Findings, investigates self-supervised repetitive action counting through period-adaptive temporal modeling. More recently, I have studied test-time computation and visual evidence selection for video-language models. In ongoing first-author work evaluated on 9,015 questions across five video benchmarks, our approach improves accuracy by 3.15 percentage points over uniform sampling at a comparable input-token cost. In a separate project on frame sufficiency and adaptive inference routing, I found that a strategy effective on Qwen2.5-VL did not transfer reliably to LLaVA-Video. These experiences have motivated a broader question: beyond determining which information a model receives, **what governs its ability to represent, integrate, and reason over that information?** The differences I observed across models have further encouraged me to investigate how such capabilities depend on the representations and computational mechanisms learned by different architectures.

One direction I hope to explore is how multimodal representations can better preserve and organize information relevant to reasoning. Visual understanding often requires more than recognizing objects or describing individual observations; it involves relationships, temporal dependencies, and changes that unfold over time. I am interested in how models encode such structure, how visual and linguistic information interact within their internal representations, and what information is preserved or lost as inputs are processed. In particular, I would like to understand whether more structured or adaptive representations can improve reasoning and generalization, and under what conditions they offer advantages over existing approaches.

A related interest concerns how these capabilities can be developed through learning and model design. Rather than treating representation quality as a fixed property of pretrained models, I hope to investigate how architectural choices, training objectives, and learning mechanisms shape the information models acquire and the ways they use it. For example, predictive or self-supervised objectives may provide opportunities to encourage models to capture relationships and transformations that are not explicitly described in language. I am also interested in how learning objectives can encourage models to integrate information across modalities and support reasoning beyond the distributions or tasks encountered during training. My aim is not to commit to a particular architecture or learning paradigm, but to understand which mechanisms lead to more transferable and reliable capabilities.

Another question I hope to pursue is how models should use computation during reasoning. My experience with video-language inference has shown me that the amount of available information and the amount of useful information are not necessarily equivalent. This motivates me to investigate how models determine what information requires further processing, when additional computation is beneficial, and how reasoning can be organized to make effective use of limited computational resources. More broadly, I am interested in the relationship between learned representations and computational processes: whether improvements in representation can reduce the need for additional inference, and whether adaptive computation can compensate for limitations in what a model has learned. I see these as complementary aspects of a larger question about how model capabilities emerge from the interaction between learning and inference.

Throughout my research, I hope to combine methodological development with careful investigation of why particular approaches succeed or fail. Beyond benchmark improvements, I value controlled comparisons across architectures, tasks, and computational budgets, together with analyses of what internal representations retain and how changes to them affect model behavior. Such investigations could help distinguish improvements that depend on specific models or evaluation settings from mechanisms that generalize more broadly. During my PhD, I hope to build on my background in visual and temporal understanding while developing deeper expertise in multimodal learning, model design, and reasoning. Ultimately, I aspire to pursue research that not only improves current systems but also contributes to a more fundamental understanding of how intelligent models learn and use information.
